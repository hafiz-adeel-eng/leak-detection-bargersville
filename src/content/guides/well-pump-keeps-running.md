Homes on private wells don't have a water meter or a monthly bill to warn them about a leak. Instead, the warning is the **pump**: it starts running more often, runs longer, or won't shut off at all. Around Trafalgar and the rural parts of Johnson County, where many homes are on wells with long lines to the house and outbuildings, that's often the first sign of a buried leak. Here's how to sort out whether it's the equipment or a leak.

## How a well system is supposed to work

A **pressure switch** turns the pump on when pressure falls to the *cut-in* setting and off when it reaches the *cut-out* setting. Common settings are **30/50** and **40/60 psi**. A **pressure tank** holds a cushion of air so the pump doesn't start every time you run a tap, and a **check valve** keeps water from draining back down the well when the pump stops.

With no water in use, pressure should hold steady and the pump should stay off. If it doesn't, something is letting pressure drop, or the switch isn't reading it correctly.

## Common causes

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Pump never shuts off, pressure stays below cut-out | Worn pump, low water in the well, a big leak, or a switch set too high |
| Pump won't shut off even at high pressure | Stuck or burned pressure switch contacts, clogged switch tube |
| Pump turns on and off every few seconds when water is used | **Waterlogged tank** or wrong tank air charge |
| Pump kicks on by itself with no water in use | **Leak** in the plumbing or line, a **leaking check valve**, or a running toilet |
| Pump runs and pressure is low, water sputters | Low well water, a failing pump, or a break in the drop pipe |

*If the pump won't stop and you can't see why, switch it off at its breaker to protect it, then work through the checks below.*

## Step 1: Watch the pressure gauge

1. Turn off every faucet, toilet fill, softener, irrigation and outdoor hydrant.
2. Let the pump reach cut-out and shut off.
3. Watch the gauge for 20–30 minutes.

**If the needle holds steady,** there's no leak in the pressurized system; look at the switch and tank. **If it slowly falls** and the pump kicks on by itself, water is escaping somewhere.

## Step 2: Check the pressure tank

With the pump off at the breaker and the water drained from the tank through a faucet:

- **Press the air valve** on top of the tank (it looks like a tire valve). If water comes out, the bladder has failed and the tank is waterlogged.
- **Check the air charge** with a tire gauge. For most bladder tanks it should be about **2 psi below the cut-in pressure**: 28 psi for a 30/50 switch, 38 psi for a 40/60 switch.

A waterlogged or undercharged tank causes rapid on-off cycling, which is hard on the pump but isn't a leak.

## Step 3: Split the system in half

If pressure drops with everything off, find out which side is losing it:

1. Close the **valve on the house side of the pressure tank**.
2. Watch the gauge again.
   - **Still drops:** the problem is between the tank and the well: a **leaking check valve**, a leak in the buried line from the well, or a leak in the drop pipe inside the well.
   - **Holds:** the leak is **in the house**: a toilet, a faucet, a water heater relief valve, a softener, or a hidden pipe.
3. Put dye in toilet tanks to rule them out, and check the softener isn't stuck in a regeneration cycle.

## Step 4: Look for signs of a buried leak

- A **wet, soft or greener** strip of ground along the route from the well to the house, or to a barn, shop or hydrant
- **Muddy or sandy water**, or air sputtering from taps
- Water around the **well casing** or where the line enters the house
- A leak that got worse after a hard freeze. Lines to detached buildings and yard hydrants are the most exposed. See [frozen pipe leaks](/guides/frozen-pipe-leaks/).

## Finding a leak in a long well line

Digging along a few hundred feet of line to look for a leak is slow and expensive. With [underground water leak detection](/services/underground-water-leak-detection/), we:

- **Trace the line** from the well to the house and any outbuildings
- **Listen along it** with a ground microphone to find where the leak noise is loudest
- Use **tracer gas** on plastic well lines, which carry sound poorly
- **Mark the spot** so only one small hole is dug

If the leak is inside the house instead, see [hear water running when nothing is on](/guides/water-running-sound-in-pipes/) and [water in the basement or crawl space](/guides/water-in-basement-or-crawl-space/).

## Who to call

- **Pump, switch, tank or check valve problems:** a licensed well and pump contractor.
- **Pressure drops and you can't see water:** leak detection to find the leak, then a plumber or well contractor to repair it.

Indiana's Department of Natural Resources keeps a public database of water well records, which can show your well's depth and construction ([IDNR water well record database](https://www.in.gov/dnr/water/ground-water-wells/water-well-record-database/)). That helps whoever works on your system.
