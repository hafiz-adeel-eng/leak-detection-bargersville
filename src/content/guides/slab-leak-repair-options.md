Once a slab leak is found, the next question is how to fix it. There are three main approaches, and each one fits a different situation. This guide explains them so you can have an informed conversation with your plumber.

> **First things first:** every option starts with knowing exactly where the leak is. See [how to find a slab leak](/guides/how-to-find-a-slab-leak/) and our [slab leak detection](/services/slab-leak-detection/) service.

## Option 1: Spot repair

**What it is:** the concrete is opened at the marked leak, the damaged section of pipe is repaired or replaced, and the slab and flooring are patched.

**Best when:**
- there's a single leak,
- the rest of the pipe is in good condition, and
- the leak location is accurately marked.

**Keep in mind:** flooring above the leak has to be removed and replaced, which matters more with tile or hardwood. Accurate detection keeps the opening small.

## Option 2: Reroute

**What it is:** the leaking line is abandoned under the slab, and a new line is run through the walls, ceiling or attic to reconnect the fixtures it served.

**Best when:**
- the leak is under an area that's hard or costly to open, like a finished kitchen or large tile floor,
- the line has leaked before, or
- you'd rather not break the slab at all.

**Keep in mind:** it means some drywall work, and the new route has to be planned around the home's layout.

## Option 3: Repipe

**What it is:** most or all of the home's water supply lines are replaced, usually rerouted overhead rather than under the slab.

**Best when:**
- the pipes are old or have had several leaks,
- there are signs of widespread wear, or
- you want to stop repairing one leak at a time.

**Keep in mind:** it's the largest job of the three, with more wall openings, but it addresses the whole system rather than one spot.

## Quick comparison

| | Spot repair | Reroute | Repipe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opens the slab | Yes, at one marked spot | No | Usually no |
| Opens walls or ceilings | No | Some | More |
| Fixes | The one leak | The leaking line | The whole supply system |
| Fits best | One leak, sound pipe | Hard-to-reach leak, repeat leaks on one line | Aging pipe, multiple leaks |

## What decides the right option

- **Pipe age and material** — older copper lines with pinholes are more likely to leak again
- **Leak history** — first leak or the third?
- **Leak location** — under a finished kitchen, a bathroom or an open area
- **Hot or cold side** — hot lines see more expansion and wear
- **Flooring above the leak** — tile and hardwood are costlier to open and replace
- **How long you plan to stay** in the home
