Polybutylene was marketed as the plumbing pipe of the future and installed in millions of homes from the late 1970s until 1995. Then it started failing. If your home was built or replumbed in that era, including many subdivisions around Greenwood, Center Grove and Whiteland, it's worth checking what your water lines are made of. Here's how to identify polybutylene, why it's a risk, and what replacing it involves.

## How to identify polybutylene

- **Material:** flexible plastic pipe
- **Color:** usually **dull gray**; sometimes **blue** or **black**
- **Size:** about **½ to 1 inch** in diameter
- **Markings:** often stamped **PB2110** along the pipe
- **Fittings:** plastic (acetal) or copper/aluminum crimp rings
- **Era:** homes built or replumbed from about **1978 to 1995**

Where to look:

- Where the **main line** enters the house
- Near the **water heater**
- Under **sinks** and behind **toilets** (supply stubs)
- In an unfinished **basement** or **crawl space**

Gray pipe going *into* the house from outside may be polybutylene service line. Note: gray PEX exists too; PEX is marked "PEX," not "PB."

## Why it fails

Polybutylene can react with **oxidants such as chlorine** in treated public water. Over time the inside of the pipe becomes **brittle** and cracks. Early plastic fittings also failed. The problem:

- Failures often come **without warning**
- They can happen **inside walls and ceilings**
- There's **no reliable way to predict** which section will fail next

A class action settlement, **Cox v. Shell Oil**, in 1995 provided relief for some homeowners, and production stopped that year. That settlement has long since closed to new claims.

## Insurance and home sales

Because of its failure history:

- **Some insurers** won't write a policy on a home with polybutylene, charge more, or exclude related damage.
- **Home inspectors** typically flag it.
- **Buyers and lenders** may ask for replacement or a price reduction.

If you're buying or selling a home from that era, identify the pipe early.

## Replacement

Polybutylene replacement is done as a **repipe**, usually with **PEX**:

- National cost guides put a whole-house repipe at roughly **$4,500 to $15,000**, with PEX at the lower end. See [whole house repipe cost](/guides/whole-house-repipe-cost/).
- A polybutylene **service line** from the meter is a separate job. See [water line replacement cost](/guides/water-line-replacement-cost-indiana/).
- Plan for **drywall patching** where walls and ceilings are opened.

## If a polybutylene pipe is leaking now

1. **Shut off the main water.** See [how to shut off the water to your house](/guides/how-to-shut-off-water-to-house/).
2. **Have a plumber repair** the failed section.
3. **Find any other hidden leaks.** One failure is often a sign of more. [Hidden water leak detection](/services/hidden-water-leak-detection/) checks for leaks inside walls and ceilings without opening them all.
4. **Get quotes** for a full repipe.
