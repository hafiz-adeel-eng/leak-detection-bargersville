When people hear "leak detection," they often picture someone chipping away at the floor until they find wet soil. Professional leak detection is the opposite: the point is to find the leak *before* anything is broken. Here's how it works, and which method fits which kind of leak.

## Step 1: Confirm and isolate the leak

Every job starts by proving there's a leak and figuring out which part of the plumbing it's in.

- **Meter test:** with all water off, any movement on the meter means water is escaping.
- **Isolation:** closing the main shutoff, the water heater inlet or the irrigation valve splits the system into sections. This quickly shows whether the leak is on the hot side, the cold side, the service line or the sprinklers.
- **Pressure testing:** a line is pressurized and watched with a gauge. A steady pressure drop confirms that line is leaking.

This stage keeps the search small. Instead of "somewhere in the house," it becomes "the hot water line under the kitchen."

## Step 2: Pinpoint the location

### Acoustic listening (ground microphones and geophones)

Water forced through a crack under pressure makes noise: a hiss or rush that travels through the pipe and the ground. A technician moves a sensitive **ground microphone** (a modern electronic *geophone*) along the pipe's path and listens through headphones. A frequency display shows where the sound peaks. The loudest point is usually right above the leak.

**Best for:** pressurized copper and metal lines under slabs and in yards.

### Leak noise correlators

A **correlator** places two sensors on the pipe at two access points, such as a meter and a hose bib. It measures how long the leak noise takes to reach each sensor and calculates the position between them.

**Best for:** longer buried runs where you can reach the pipe at two points.

### Tracer gas

When a leak is too quiet to hear, often in plastic pipe, the line is drained and filled with a safe tracer gas, typically a mix of 5% hydrogen and 95% nitrogen. Hydrogen molecules are tiny and rise through soil and concrete, and a sensitive detector "sniffs" for them at the surface.

**Best for:** PEX, PVC and poly lines, very small leaks, and noisy environments.

### Thermal imaging

An infrared camera shows surface temperature. A hot water leak under a floor shows up as a warm bloom, and wet drywall often reads cooler than dry drywall. Thermal imaging doesn't see *through* walls. It shows the temperature pattern a leak leaves on the surface, which is then confirmed with a moisture meter.

**Best for:** hot water slab leaks, leaks inside walls and ceilings, and water intrusion.

### Moisture meters

Pin and pinless moisture meters measure how wet drywall, wood and flooring are. Mapping readings across a wall or floor shows where the water is spreading from.

**Best for:** confirming thermal images and mapping damage.

### Hydrophones (pools)

An underwater listening device picks up the sound of water being pulled through a crack or a leaking fitting in a pool or spa.

**Best for:** pool shells, returns, skimmers and main drains.

### Ground penetrating radar (GPR)

GPR sends radar pulses into the ground and reads the reflections. It can help locate buried pipes and disturbed or saturated soil, but it doesn't "see" leaks directly. It's used alongside other methods, not instead of them.

## What happens after the leak is found

The technician marks the spot, often with tape or paint on the floor or a flag in the yard, and documents it with photos and readings. The repair is a separate step, usually done by a plumber, who opens only the marked area. Detection and repair are different skills. Accurate detection is what keeps the repair small.

## Why accurate leak detection matters

- **Less demolition:** one small opening instead of exploratory holes in tile, concrete or lawn.
- **Lower repair cost:** less flooring, concrete and drywall to replace.
- **Faster repair:** the plumber starts in the right place.
- **Documentation:** photos and readings are useful for your insurer.
