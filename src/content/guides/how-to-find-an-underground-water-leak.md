Knowing **how to find a water leak underground** saves you from the worst outcome: a trench across the lawn, dug by guesswork. The buried water service line runs from the meter to your house, out of sight, and a leak in it can drain into the soil for months before anyone notices. The process below works in a fixed order. First prove there is a leak, then prove it is outside the house, then find where the pipe runs, and only then pinpoint the spot.

Leaks are common. The EPA's WaterSense program estimates that household leaks waste nearly 10,000 gallons a year in the average home, and that about one home in ten has leaks wasting 90 gallons or more a day. Underground leaks are among the hardest to spot because the water never reaches the floor.

## How to find a water leak underground: the 6 steps

1. **Confirm the leak at the water meter** with every fixture off.
2. **Close the main shutoff** in the house to tell an inside leak from an outside one.
3. **Rule out the sprinkler system** and any other line that branches off underground.
4. **Find where the service line runs**, from the meter to where it enters the house.
5. **Walk the line and read the ground** for soft, sunken or greener spots.
6. **Pinpoint the leak before anyone digs**, with acoustic listening, a correlator or tracer gas.

Steps 1 to 5 need no tools beyond a flashlight and a meter key. Step 6 needs detection equipment.

## What counts as an underground water leak

An underground leak is any pressurized water line leaking below ground outside the house. That usually means one of these:

- **The water service line.** This is the buried pipe from the meter or curb stop to the house. It is the most common source.
- **The irrigation mainline.** It tees off the service line, often near the meter or the house, and stays pressurized all the time.
- **Branch lines** to a detached garage, barn, pool equipment pad or yard hydrant.
- **A well line**, the pipe from the well head to the pressure tank on homes with a private well.

Four parts of the system matter for the search:

- **Water meter:** where the utility measures your use, usually in a pit near the street.
- **Curb stop:** the utility's shutoff valve near the property line.
- **Main shutoff valve:** your valve where the line enters the house.
- **Service line:** the pipe between the meter and the main shutoff, buried below the frost line.

## Signs of a water leak underground

| Sign | What it usually means | How strong a clue |
|---|---|---|
| Meter or leak indicator moves with all water off | Water is escaping somewhere | Strong: proves a leak |
| Soggy, spongy or sunken strip of lawn | Water is saturating the soil above or near the line | Strong if it lines up with the pipe |
| Grass greener or faster-growing in a line | A steady supply of water below the root zone | Medium, most visible in dry weeks |
| Water at the meter pit, a driveway joint or the foundation | Water is surfacing where the soil gives way | Medium: it may have travelled |
| Lower pressure in the whole house | Water is lost before it reaches the fixtures | Medium: a bad pressure valve does the same |
| Bill higher with no change in use | Continuous flow through the meter | Medium, slow to show |
| Air sputtering or cloudy water after a repair elsewhere | Air or soil entering a damaged line | Weak on its own |

No single sign proves where the leak is. Together, the meter test and one or two surface signs point to the buried line.

## Step 1: Confirm the leak at the water meter

Turn off every faucet, the dishwasher, the washer, the ice maker, the softener (put it in bypass) and the sprinkler controller. Then open the meter pit lid and look at the dial.

- **Leak indicator:** most residential meters have a small triangle, star or gear that spins with even a trickle of flow. If it turns, water is moving.
- **Reading test:** write down the full reading, wait 15 to 30 minutes without using water, and read it again. Any change is a leak.

A small leak adds up. Just one gallon a minute is 1,440 gallons a day, or more than 43,000 gallons a month. The meter is also the only test that catches a leak you can't see, hear or feel.

## Step 2: Close the main shutoff to split inside from outside

Close the main shutoff valve where the line enters the house. It is often in a basement, a crawl space, a utility room, near the water heater or in the garage. Then watch the meter for another 15 to 30 minutes.

| Meter with the house shut off | Where the leak is |
|---|---|
| Stops | Inside the house: fixtures, walls or [under the slab](/guides/how-to-find-a-slab-leak/) |
| Keeps moving | Outside: the service line, the irrigation mainline or another buried branch |
| Slows but doesn't stop | Possibly two leaks, or a main shutoff that doesn't seal fully |

If the shutoff is old and won't close all the way, the result is not reliable. See [how to shut off water to your house](/guides/how-to-shut-off-water-to-house/) for stuck valves.

## Step 3: Rule out the sprinkler system and branch lines

An irrigation mainline leak looks exactly like a service line leak on the meter, because both sit between the meter and the house. Close the irrigation isolation valve, usually in the basement or in a valve box near the backflow preventer, and check the meter again.

- **Meter stops** with irrigation closed: the leak is in the sprinkler system. Follow [how to find a sprinkler leak](/guides/how-to-find-a-sprinkler-leak/).
- **Meter keeps moving:** the leak is still in the service line or another branch, such as a line to a garage or yard hydrant. Close those branches one at a time if they have valves.

## Step 4: Find where the service line runs

You can't find a leak on a pipe you can't locate. The service line usually runs from the meter pit toward the point where the pipe comes through the foundation, but it is often not a straight line. It may bend around trees, follow an old driveway, or come in at a different wall than you'd expect.

Ways to work out the path:

- **Look at both ends.** Note where the line leaves the meter pit and where it enters the house, then picture the shortest path that avoids obstacles.
- **Check records.** Builders' plot plans, the utility's records, or an old inspection report sometimes show the route.
- **Use a pipe locator.** On metal pipe, a transmitter clamped to the line sends a signal you can follow from the surface. Plastic pipe needs a tracer wire (if one was buried with it) or a sonde pushed into the line.
- **Call 811 before any digging.** Indiana 811 marks gas, electric and cable lines, not your private water line, but you need those marks before a single shovel goes in.

Ground penetrating radar can also show where a pipe runs. It is a location tool first, not a leak finder.

## Step 5: Walk the line and read the ground

With the path roughly known, walk it slowly, ideally a day or two after dry weather.

- Press the lawn with your foot every few feet. A spot that feels spongy or squishes is a clue.
- Look for a sunken strip, a hollow under a sidewalk slab, or a driveway joint that stays dark and damp.
- In late summer, look for a band of grass that stays green when the rest of the lawn has browned.
- Check the meter pit. Water standing in it on a dry day, with clear water flowing in, often means a leak near the meter.

**Keep in mind that water travels.** It runs along the gravel bedding around the pipe and follows the easiest path through the soil. The wet spot can sit several feet downhill of the real break, so treat it as a starting point, not a dig site.

## Step 6: Pinpoint the leak before anyone digs

This is where detection equipment replaces guesswork. The goal is a mark on the ground within inches of the leak, so the repair needs one small hole.

| Method | How it works | Best for | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acoustic ground microphone | A sensitive microphone on the surface picks up the hiss of water escaping under pressure; the sound peaks over the leak | Copper and galvanized lines, moderate depth | Plastic pipe and deep or wet soil muffle the sound |
| Leak noise correlator | Sensors at two access points (the meter and a hose bib, for example) time the leak noise and calculate its position | Longer metal lines with access at both ends | Needs two contact points and a known pipe length |
| Tracer gas | The drained line is filled with a safe mix of about 5% hydrogen and 95% nitrogen; the gas rises through the soil and a detector finds it above the leak | Polyethylene, PVC and PEX lines, quiet leaks | The line must be isolated; very wet soil slows the gas |
| Pressure test | The isolated line is pressurized and watched on a gauge | Confirming which line leaks | Shows that a line leaks, not where |
| Pipe and cable locating | A transmitter signal traces the pipe's route from the surface | Mapping the route before pinpointing | Plastic pipe needs a tracer wire or sonde |

A professional usually combines them: a pressure test to confirm the line, locating to map it, then acoustic listening or tracer gas to pinpoint. Read more in [leak detection methods](/guides/leak-detection-methods/).

## Your pipe material changes the search

| Pipe material | Common in | How it fails underground | Best pinpointing method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copper (type K) | Many homes from the 1950s to 1990s | Pinholes, corrosion at soil contact, frost damage | Acoustic listening, correlation |
| Galvanized steel | Older homes, before about 1960 | Rusts from inside, leaks at threaded joints | Acoustic listening |
| Polyethylene (PE, poly) | Most newer service lines | Cracked fittings, joints pulled apart by soil movement | Tracer gas |
| PVC | Some service lines and most irrigation mains | Cracked glued joints, damage from roots or loads | Tracer gas, acoustic at close range |
| PEX | Newer installs | Failed fittings, damage during landscaping | Tracer gas |

If you don't know your pipe, look where it comes through the foundation or at the meter. Copper is orange-brown, galvanized steel is gray with threaded joints, and plastic is black, blue or white.

## What you can do yourself, and when to call

You can do steps 1 to 5 yourself: the meter test, the shutoff test, ruling out irrigation, finding the route and reading the ground. Those steps tell you whether the leak is underground and roughly where. They also give a professional a head start.

Call for underground water leak detection when:

- the meter keeps moving with the house and irrigation shut off,
- the line runs under a driveway, patio or mature landscaping, where a wrong guess is expensive,
- the line is plastic, which rarely makes a sound you can hear,
- the wet spot is far from where the pipe should be.

What not to do: don't dig where the grass is wettest and hope. Without pinpointing, that often means several holes, damaged landscaping and sometimes a cut irrigation or cable line. See [water leak under the driveway](/guides/water-leak-under-driveway/) if the line runs under concrete.

## Who is responsible for an underground water leak

In most areas, the homeowner owns the service line from the meter, or the curb stop, to the house. The utility owns the meter and the pipe on the street side of it. If water is coming up in the meter pit itself or between the meter and the street, call the utility first. A leak on your side of the meter is your repair, and many utilities will consider a [water bill leak adjustment](/guides/water-bill-leak-adjustment-indiana/) once it's fixed.

## Finding underground leaks in Johnson County soil

Johnson County sits on glacial clay that holds water rather than draining it. That changes how an underground leak looks here. Instead of a gusher, a leak on a buried service line usually shows up as a strip of lawn that stays soft and green in August, or as clear water seeping into the meter pit. Clay also expands when wet and shrinks when dry, which works on fittings and joints over time. And because central Indiana's frost depth is about 30 to 36 inches, service lines are buried deep enough that the water often surfaces well away from the break.

Homes on larger lots around Trafalgar, Franklin and rural Bargersville often have long service lines, sometimes a hundred feet or more from the meter, where pinpointing matters most. Homes on private wells around Trafalgar, Morgantown and Nineveh have no utility meter, so the well pump that runs or cycles with no water in use is the first clue; see [well pump keeps running](/guides/well-pump-keeps-running/). Whichever utility serves you, whether Indiana American Water, the Bargersville utility, Whiteland Water Works or Citizens Water in Southport, the meter test above works the same.

Before any excavation, Indiana law requires a call to Indiana 811 at least two full working days ahead. Pinpointing the leak first keeps that excavation to one small hole.
