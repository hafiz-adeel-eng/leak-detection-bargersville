A water bill that suddenly jumps is often the first sign of a leak, but not always. It can also be the sprinklers or a billing change. Work through these nine checks, easiest first, to find out which.

## First: read the bill, not just the total

Look at the **usage in gallons** (or cubic feet) and compare it with the same month last year and the last few months. Also check:

- **Was the reading actual or estimated?** An estimated bill followed by an actual reading can make one month look huge.
- **Did the rates change?** A rate increase raises the total even when usage is the same.
- **How many days does the bill cover?** A longer billing period means a bigger bill.

If the **gallons** went up, keep going.

## 1. Do the water meter test

Turn off all water inside and out, note the meter reading and the leak indicator, and wait one to two hours. The U.S. EPA recommends a two-hour test ([EPA WaterSense](https://www.epa.gov/watersense/fix-leak-week)). If the meter moves, there's a leak. The rest of this list helps you find it.

## 2. Check every toilet

A worn flapper or a fill valve set too high can let a toilet run silently all day. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank and wait 10 minutes without flushing. Color in the bowl means a leak.

## 3. Look for dripping faucets and showerheads

A small drip adds up. The EPA notes that a faucet dripping once per second can waste more than 3,000 gallons a year.

## 4. Check the water heater

Look for water around the base, a discharge pipe from the relief valve that drips, or damp fittings. A water heater that runs more than usual can also point to a hot water leak elsewhere.

## 5. Check the water softener

A softener stuck in a regeneration cycle can send water to the drain for hours. Listen for water running to the drain when it shouldn't be.

## 6. Check appliances and supply lines

Washing machine hoses, the dishwasher connection, the refrigerator's ice-maker line, and supply lines under sinks.

## 7. Check outdoor spigots and hoses

A hose left connected to a spigot, or a frost-free spigot that cracked over the winter, can leak into the wall or the ground.

## 8. Check the sprinkler system

Irrigation is often a home's biggest water use in summer. A broken head, a stuck valve, or a mainline leak can waste a lot of water. Close the irrigation isolation valve and repeat the meter test. See [how to find a sprinkler leak](/guides/how-to-find-a-sprinkler-leak/).

## 9. Consider a hidden leak

If everything above checks out and the meter still moves with all water off, the leak is hidden:

- **Under the slab.** Look for warm spots on the floor. See [how to find a slab leak](/guides/how-to-find-a-slab-leak/).
- **Inside a wall.** Look for stains, bubbling paint or a musty smell. See [how to find a leak in a wall](/guides/how-to-find-a-leak-in-a-wall/).
- **In the buried service line.** Close the main shutoff. If the meter still moves, the leak is outside. See [how to find an underground water leak](/guides/how-to-find-an-underground-water-leak/).

## Quick reference

| What you notice | Most likely cause |
|---|---|
| Bill jumped, gallons didn't | Rate change, estimated reading or a longer billing period |
| Gallons up in summer only | Irrigation use or a sprinkler leak |
| Toilet refills on its own | Flapper or fill valve leak |
| Meter moves, nothing visible | Hidden leak: slab, wall or service line |
| Warm floor + high bill | Hot water slab leak |

## Can you get a bill adjustment?

Many water utilities offer a one-time leak adjustment once a leak is repaired. Ask your utility about their policy and keep the repair receipt and any leak detection report.
