A pinhole leak is exactly what it sounds like: a hole in a copper pipe so small you can barely see it. It can spray a fine mist inside a wall, drip onto a ceiling, or soak the soil under a slab for months. And where there's one, there are often more. Here's what causes them, how to find them, and when to stop repairing and start replacing.

## What causes pinhole leaks

Pinholes start as **pitting corrosion on the inside** of the pipe. Common drivers:

- **Water chemistry:** pH, hardness, dissolved minerals and disinfectants like chlorine or chloramine all affect how copper corrodes. Central Indiana's hard water is one factor, but chemistry varies by utility and over time.
- **High water velocity:** water moving too fast, especially in recirculating hot water lines or undersized pipe, scours the pipe wall. This is called erosion corrosion.
- **Turbulence:** at elbows, tees and poorly reamed cut ends.
- **Thin-wall pipe:** Type M copper has thinner walls than Types L and K, so it has less metal to lose.
- **High water pressure:** increases velocity and stress.
- **Stray current or poor grounding:** can accelerate corrosion in some cases.

## Signs of a pinhole leak

- A **tiny spray or drip** on an exposed pipe, often with green or blue staining
- **Stains or bubbling paint** on a wall or ceiling
- A **water meter that moves** with everything off
- A **hissing sound** in a wall or under the floor. See [hear water running when nothing is on](/guides/water-running-sound-in-pipes/).
- **Warm spots on the floor** from a hot-side pinhole under a slab
- A **higher water bill**

## Finding a hidden pinhole

A pinhole in an exposed basement pipe is easy to spot. One inside a wall or under a slab is not, and it makes very little water at first, so the damage builds slowly.

With [slab leak detection](/services/slab-leak-detection/) and hidden leak detection, we:

1. **Confirm** the leak with a meter test and isolate the hot, cold and service lines.
2. **Pressure-test** the suspect line.
3. **Listen** with acoustic equipment for the hiss of the pinhole.
4. **Scan** with a thermal camera for the warm or wet pattern.
5. **Mark** the spot for the plumber.

See [how to find a slab leak](/guides/how-to-find-a-slab-leak/) for the signs and home tests.

## Repair options

| Option | When it fits |
|---|---|
| **Repair clamp** | Temporary fix on an exposed pipe until a plumber arrives |
| **Cut out and replace the section** | One pinhole in a line that's otherwise in good shape |
| **Reroute the line** | Pinhole in a slab line; bypasses the old line through walls or the attic |
| **Repipe** | Several pinholes in a few years, thin or badly pitted pipe throughout |

Epoxy putty and tape are emergency stopgaps, not repairs.

## When to stop repairing

Pinholes come from conditions that affect the **whole system**, not one spot. A good rule of thumb:

- **One pinhole:** repair it, and keep an eye on the meter.
- **Two or more in a few years,** or a plumber finds thin, pitted pipe when making the repair: get a quote to **reroute or repipe**. See [whole house repipe cost](/guides/whole-house-repipe-cost/).
- **Pinholes under the slab** more than once: rerouting overhead usually beats repeated slab repairs. See [slab leak repair cost](/guides/slab-leak-repair-cost/).

## Reduce the risk

- **Check your water pressure.** Keep it under 80 psi with a working pressure-reducing valve.
- **Ask your utility** about water chemistry changes if pinholes start appearing across your neighborhood.
- **Have a plumber check** that hot water recirculation pumps aren't running too fast.
- **Get pinholes found early,** before water damage spreads.
