Central Indiana basements rely on sump pumps, especially in spring and after long wet spells when the clay-rich soil stays saturated. A pump that runs a lot during heavy rain is doing its job. A pump that runs constantly in dry weather, or never shuts off, is telling you something is wrong: with the pump, the pit, or a source of water that shouldn't be there. Here's how to sort it out.

## Quick checks: is it the pump?

| Check | What to look for | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Float switch** | Float stuck up against the pit wall, the pump, or debris | Free it; reposition the pump; clean the pit |
| **Check valve** | Water rushing back into the pit right after the pump stops | Replace the check valve |
| **Discharge line** | Frozen, clogged or crushed pipe; water not coming out outside | Clear or thaw it; protect the outlet |
| **Pump size / pit size** | Small pit refilling in seconds, pump that can't keep up | Larger pit liner or a properly sized pump |
| **Pump condition** | Humming, running with little water moving | Worn impeller or motor; replace the pump |

*A pump that never stops can overheat and burn out. If yours won't shut off and the pit is empty, unplug it while you check the float, and plug it back in once it's fixed.*

## Where is the water coming from?

If the pump itself is fine, the question is **why the pit keeps filling**.

| When it runs | Likely source |
|---|---|
| During and after rain or snowmelt | **Surface water or groundwater**: normal, within limits |
| Days or weeks after rain has stopped | **High water table**, poor grading or downspouts dumping near the foundation |
| Dry weather, steady clear inflow | **A leaking pressurized water line** near the foundation |
| When sprinklers run, or shortly after | **An irrigation leak** |
| Only when the washer or a sink drains | Drain water routed or leaking into the pit (check with a plumber) |

## Test for a plumbing leak

1. **Meter test:** turn off every faucet, appliance and the irrigation system, then watch the meter's leak indicator for 15–30 minutes. If it moves, a line is leaking.
2. **Close the main shutoff** in the house. If the meter still moves, the leak is in the **buried service line** between the meter and the house, often right where it comes through the foundation near the pit.
3. **Close the irrigation valve** separately to rule sprinklers in or out.
4. **Watch the pit** with the water off. If the inflow slows over a few hours after you shut off the main at the meter (if allowed), that points to your water line rather than groundwater.

Homes on a private well won't have a meter; watch the pump and pressure gauge instead. See [well pump keeps running](/guides/well-pump-keeps-running/).

## Why a service line leak ends up in the sump pit

The water service line is buried below the frost line and usually enters the house through the foundation wall or floor. When it leaks, water follows the pipe trench, which is loose, easy-draining backfill, straight toward the foundation. Around the house, it reaches the drain tile and ends up in the sump pit. The pump runs, the water bill climbs, and nothing looks wet outside.

## Reduce outside water

If the water is groundwater or runoff:

- Extend **downspouts** well away from the foundation
- Regrade soil so it **slopes away** from the house
- Clear **gutters** and window wells
- Make sure the **discharge** empties far enough from the house that it doesn't flow back
